Faster Results, Same Standards: What I Ask of Students in AI for Science

AI has lowered the cost of producing a result by a large factor. It has not lowered the bar for a good one. This is how I currently think about what the tools change and what they leave untouched, and the concrete things I ask of students in my group before a result counts.

Ruixiang (Ryan) Tang  ·  Rutgers University  ·  November 2026

Can a student who uses AI tools from the first week of a PhD still become a strong scientist? I hear versions of this question from colleagues, from prospective students, and sometimes from myself.

The tools are now part of daily research. They write code, summarize papers, propose candidate molecules, fit surrogate models for simulations, and draft text. Work that used to take a semester can take a week. Alongside this, a worry has become common: that students are learning to produce results without learning how to do science, and that a generation of researchers will end up operating models they do not understand.

I think the worry points at something real, but the answer is not to stay away from the tools. The answer is to be clear about what the tools change and what they leave untouched. This post has two parts. The first is how I currently think about AI for Science. The second is the concrete set of things I ask of students in my group. Little of it is new. Most of it is what good advisors asked for long before these tools existed. What has changed is how easy it has become to skip each step.

What AI changes, and what it does not

The cost of a result dropped. The bar for a good one did not.

AI has lowered the cost of producing a result by a large factor. A screening run, a trained predictor, a plotted curve, a first draft: each takes a fraction of the time it used to. But the bar for a good result is where it was. It should be correct. It should be explainable to someone outside the project. Someone else should be able to reproduce it. And it should answer a question worth answering.

The shift

When producing results gets cheap, judgment becomes the scarce part. Which problem is worth a year of your life. Which of twenty promising curves to believe. Which direction to drop after two weeks.

These decisions were the core of research before AI. They are now a larger share of it, because so much of the remaining work has become fast.

A good prediction is not yet an explanation

A model that predicts well has learned something, but it does not tell us what it learned or why it works. Science cares about mechanism. A structure predictor, a property model for materials, or a classifier on cell images can be very useful and still leave the scientific question open.

I prefer to treat a strong prediction as a hypothesis. It tells us where to look. The next step is to test it, with an experiment, with theory, or by opening up the model and checking what it relies on. Interpretability is one bridge here, and it is part of why my group works on it. A model that is right for the wrong reason tends to be wrong later in a way that matters.

The interesting part is often where the model fails

Models trained on one distribution often fail quietly on another, and they tend to fail with confidence. A model that is accurate on held-out data from the same lab, instrument, or time period can degrade badly on data from somewhere else, with no warning in its output.

Where a method breaks, and why, is often the most informative thing we can learn about it. I do not think of reliability as a step added after the research is done. It is part of the research. A paper that reports clearly where its method stops working tells the next person more than a paper that only reports where it works.

Everyone is using the same tools

When many groups use the same foundation models, the same pipelines, and the same public datasets, the papers start to look alike. Each one adds a small increment over the last. This is not a failure of any individual student. It is what happens when the same tools are pointed at the same problems.

The differences that last usually come from something others do not have. A dataset collected with care. A long collaboration with domain scientists who know where the hard problems are. A question that others have not yet noticed is a question. These are slower to build than a new fine-tuning run, and they are worth more.

Generating got easier. Checking did not.

It has become easy to generate a result that looks reasonable. It has not become easier to confirm that the result is true. The volume of plausible output makes checking more important, not less.

Wet-lab validation, independent replication, careful comparison against simple baselines, formal verification where it applies, and sitting down with a domain expert to go through results line by line all carry more weight now. In my own work I have seen how easily a near-perfect number can come from the way the data was collected rather than from anything the model learned [1]. That kind of mistake is easy to make and slow to catch, and fast tools make it easier to make at scale.

What I ask of students

These are the things I look for when a student in my group brings me a result. None of them is about avoiding AI. Each one is about keeping the thinking with the person.

01Derive it before you call it

Before you use a model or a tool, understand what it is doing. Where did the training data come from? What is the loss actually optimizing? What does the method assume about its inputs? I would like you to be able to explain the method on a whiteboard, not only call its API.

This does not mean rebuilding every tool from scratch. It means knowing enough to recognize when a tool is being used outside the conditions it was built for. In scientific domains, that is where many of the silent errors come from.

02Ask how well you would do without the model

Every result needs a comparison, and the comparison should include the simplest baseline you can think of. A linear model. A rule of thumb a domain expert would use. A feature that requires no learning at all.

If a simple baseline gets you most of the way, we need to know that before anyone else points it out. In one replication we ran, the hardcoded solutions were much shorter than the honest ones, and counting characters alone reached an AUC of 0.950, with no model involved [1]. That single check changed how we read the whole result. Ablations matter for the same reason. When a pipeline has five components, you should be able to say which ones carry the result.

03Go looking for where it breaks

After a method works, try to make it fail. Change the distribution. Move to a different instrument, a different site, or a different time period. Add noise or small perturbations. See whether the result still holds.

Report what you find, including the failures. Writing clearly where a method does not work is as useful to readers as showing where it does, and reviewers increasingly ask for it.

04Look at the raw data yourself

Before training anything, look at a few dozen raw examples. Label some of them yourself if you can. Read the metadata. Check how the positive and negative examples were collected, and whether they were collected the same way.

Many problems show up in the data long before they show up in a metric. A dataset that mixes two imaging modalities, two labeling protocols, or two sources with different preprocessing can produce a strong number that has little to do with the scientific question. Ten minutes of looking can save months.

05Use AI to think faster, not to think for you

Using language models to write code, revise text, or search the literature is fine, and I use them myself. The line I draw is about ownership. You should be able to explain every line of code you submit and every claim in a paper you write, and you are responsible for the errors in both. If a model wrote a function you cannot explain, it is not ready to be part of a result.

When tools played a meaningful role, say so in the paper or the code. This is becoming standard practice, and it helps readers judge the work.

06Ask why this problem is ours

When choosing a project, ask what we bring that others do not. It might be a dataset, a collaborator, a method, or a way of looking at the problem. If the honest answer is that we would rerun a known pipeline on a new dataset, the project deserves more thought before it takes up a year.

This question matters more now than it did a few years ago. When tools are shared, the advantage moves to problems, data, and collaborations.

07Make your result easy to rerun

Keep the code, configurations, random seeds, data versions, and experiment logs. A good target is that another student in the group can reproduce your main result within a day. This is not bureaucracy. It is how you find your own bugs, and it is the first thing a careful reviewer or collaborator will test.

08Build one skill that does not expire with the next model

Tools change every few months. Some foundations last much longer: mathematics and statistics, depth in one scientific domain, or systems and engineering skill. I would like each student to be strong in at least one of these, in addition to being fluent with current tools.

A student whose main skill is fine-tuning the current generation of models will need to relearn a lot when the next generation arrives. A student who understands the underlying science or mathematics will be able to put each new generation to good use.

09Write the claim before you run the experiment

Before starting a set of experiments, write one paragraph: what you expect to show, why it matters, and what result would prove you wrong. If you cannot write that paragraph, the plan usually is not ready.

Writing it down also guards against a quiet failure that fast tools make easier: running many variations and reporting the one that looks best. A claim written in advance is a simple check on that.

A point against myself

I do not meet all of these every time. Under a deadline, it is tempting to accept a clean curve and move on, and I have felt that pull as much as anyone. Writing these down is partly a way of holding myself to the same standard I ask of students.

What it comes down to

None of these requirements is really about AI. Good research training asked for most of them before these tools existed. What AI changes is the cost of skipping them. It is now very easy to produce something that looks like a result without doing the thinking that makes it one.

The goal of a PhD is judgment: knowing which problems are worth working on, which results to trust, and when to stop and rethink.

AI can make a researcher with good judgment much more productive. It does not supply the judgment. That part is still built one careful project at a time.

If you are a student in my group, or thinking about joining, I am glad to talk about any of this. I am still learning it too.
